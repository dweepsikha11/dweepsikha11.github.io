Who am I?

Childhood and the Weight of Worth

Growing up, I was an underconfident kid, always anxious about my worth as a person. I was good at studies, often coming first in class. Mostly because when I came first, people clapped, showered compliments—external validation became my measure of worth.

At the same time, I worked hard simply because I couldn’t stand holding back my best effort. Even today, this is a challenge: sometimes I don’t realize how much to put into work. If my superior isn’t satisfied, my anxiety spikes—losing sleep, eating badly, all for work.

Was it because I wanted to give my best, or because I feared criticism? I still don’t fully know. Failing a test for the first time was a rude awakening, and even today, I struggle to deal with failure.

Heartbreak and Identity Lost

The first time I truly confronted the depth of the question “Who am I?” was after my heartbreak. The experience was earth-shattering—painful, draining, exhausting both mentally and physically.

When the relationship ended, I felt stripped of identity. The weight of that loss raised questions so overwhelming that I felt suffocated. In a world where relationships are treated like disposable fashion, my grief seemed trivial to others. Yet for me, it was profound—losing someone who had become my entire world felt like losing life itself.

People often told me, “So what? Just get over it.” But it wasn’t that simple. I had completely lost myself in it. And yet, I don’t regret it—because to love like that was as beautiful as anything in the world.

The Existential Question

In the process of working through pain, I realized something unsettling: there was no “me” anymore. My identity wasn’t just shaken—it was fractured at the core. That was the moment I began asking the hardest questions: • Who am I? • What is life for? • Why does this world exist? Pain, I’ve learned, can be more valuable than happiness. For those of us who don’t fit neatly into society’s rules, pain offers a deeper perspective on life. That emptiness pushed me to ask harder, more profound questions about myself and my existence.

Living Between Roles and Expressions

I pose as an IT professional, though I have no zeal for it. I hold opinions I don’t know are true. I seek love, unsure if it’s even real. I uphold values, uncertain if they are valuable. But I paint because I want to. I write because I want to express. And maybe, that’s where the answer lies—not in certainty, but in the freedom to create, to question, and to feel.
